I am Miette, and I want to show you how one living gesture can travel into a film without the Factory pretending it owns the person, the gesture, or the recording.

Imagine someone moving, singing, speaking, tapping a table, or making a shape with their hands while a scene is being filmed. A camera may see it. A microphone may hear it. A phone sensor may notice movement. A musician may later feel that the gesture could inspire a rhythm, a melody, or a texture. The important question is not only “Can we record it?” It is also “Who keeps it, what did they agree to, and who may decide what it becomes?”

We call the first place the field-kit. It is a real kit: camera, microphone or recorder, headphones or meters, phone sensors, maybe a MIDI or OSC controller, a slate or shared clock, power, storage, and a small local network. T10, Embodied Performance & Capture, looks after where those things are placed, whether they are working, whether their clocks agree, and where their recordings stay.

Field-kit has a second meaning too. Episode 120 has an App Workspace, a place for the relations around the work. It can remember that a take belongs to an episode, that a consent decision exists, that a composition may be related, and that an editor may have a place for a cue. This workspace does not need to swallow the recording. The physical kit and the App Workspace are like two maps of the same journey: one follows devices and bodies; the other follows responsibilities and decisions.

Between them is a small receipt called `@miadi/expressive-take`. A receipt is not the thing you bought, and this receipt is not the performance. It holds safe facts: when the take started and ended, which clock was used, what rate a sensor was asked to use and what rate it actually reached, whether each channel was complete or partial, and the fingerprints called hashes that let us recognize files without putting those files inside the receipt. It carries references to consent and custody, but it carries no participant words, names, recordings, passwords, or secret paths.

The receipt can be started, completed, held, linked, or failed. Held matters. It means the Factory is allowed to stop and wait when consent, technical trust, or artistic choice is not ready. Linked happens only after a composer or creator accepts musical material and an editor accepts a timeline guide. A computer may check the receipt, but it cannot make either artistic decision.

Episode 044 teaches us to say what kind of statement we are making. “The sensor reported 29 readings each second” is observed. “We want a soft Shared Spark cue after the shared-meaning clip” is a desired future. “This gesture feels like a heartbeat” is an interpretation. “The cue is accepted” is a human choice. Keeping these different protects imagination without disguising it as proof.

Episode 257 carries the creative lineage called Shared Spark, the album connected with William and Jerry. Its PT1 thread says this is where the music Episode 044’s film asks for may come from. Miadi issue 1035 calls that wish a MusicalDesire and describes how it could travel from the film workspace to the composer’s workspace and later return as a ScoreCue. This is a beautiful direction, but today it is still a direction. It does not mean the cue has already been composed, chosen, or placed.

Before anyone tries the real Episode 120 take, the team can rehearse safely with Episode 575. That episode already has two checked Kdenlive scene stringouts and an editing-room sheet. The team can point a practice receipt at one guide, replay its events, and prove that bad paths, hidden recordings, or secret words are refused. The rehearsal changes no Episode 575 media and claims no musical choice.

Ten teams help the passage, but each has a small job. T10 tends the body, devices, clocks, placement, monitoring, fallback, and custody. T8 builds the receipt and checks. T5 prepares context and consent. T9 carries story direction. T6 helps composer and editor meet in the timeline. T3 witnesses the creator’s experience. T1 makes the handoff resumable. The other teams carry events, charts, and operator readiness. Five circles ask whether Story, Stewardship, Engineering, Evaluation, and Continuity are each cared for. The circles are gates, not new names pasted over the teams.

Four human choices must remain separate. The participant or keeper decides permitted use. An evaluator judges whether the technical evidence is reliable. The creator or composer decides whether the take is musically useful and what it becomes. The editor or director decides whether and where it belongs in the film. Saying “no,” “not yet,” or “hold” is a successful protection, not a failed ceremony.

We will know the pilot has lived, not just been described, when one real consented take is verified, one composition is linked, one timeline placement is accepted, the human decisions are recorded, and another agent can replay the receipt and resume the work without reading the first session. Until then T10 remains a pilot and its permanent lead remains a human decision.

Your next action is to point to the journey and tell a friend: “The recording stays with its keeper; the receipt carries safe facts; people decide what the gesture becomes.”
